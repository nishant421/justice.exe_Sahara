# Landmark Judgment Principles — Sexual Offences and Child Protection

## Vishaka v State of Rajasthan (1997) — Workplace Sexual Harassment

Case: Vishaka and Others v State of Rajasthan
Year: 1997
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Employer Duty — Workplace Sexual Harassment

Principle: Sexual harassment at the workplace violates the fundamental rights of women under Articles 14 (equality), 15 (non-discrimination), 19(1)(g) (right to practice any profession), and 21 (right to life with dignity) of the Indian Constitution. Employers have a constitutional duty to provide a safe workplace free from sexual harassment. The Supreme Court issued the Vishaka Guidelines — a binding framework requiring employers to constitute Complaints Committees, adopt sexual harassment policies, and provide a redressal mechanism. These guidelines were codified into the POSH Act 2013.

Applicable scenarios: Workplace harassment; ICC failures; Employer non-compliance with POSH; Women denied safe working conditions; Hostile work environment claims.

## Independent Thought v Union of India (2017) — Child Marriage and POCSO

Case: Independent Thought v Union of India
Year: 2017
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Minor Wife — POCSO Protection — Child Marriage

Principle: Exception 2 to Section 375 IPC (marital rape exception for wives above 15) is unconstitutional insofar as it relates to girls below 18. Sexual intercourse with a wife who is a minor (below 18) constitutes rape under POCSO. Marital status does not remove POCSO protections. A child cannot consent to sexual acts regardless of whether she is married. This ruling closed the marital rape exception for minor wives and affirmed that age — not marital status — determines POCSO applicability.

Applicable scenarios: Child marriage involving sexual intercourse with minor; POCSO prosecution in context of marriage; Intersectionality of child marriage and sexual abuse; Marital rape of minors.

## Nipun Saxena v Union of India (2018) — Victim Identity Protection

Case: Nipun Saxena v Union of India
Year: 2018
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Identity Protection of Victims of Sexual Offences

Principle: The identity of victims of sexual assault (including POCSO cases) must be strictly protected at all stages — investigation, trial, media reporting, and even in court judgments. No person may publish, broadcast, or disclose any information — including name, locality, photograph, family details, school — that could lead to the identification of the victim. Courts, police, and media are all bound. Section 228A IPC criminalises disclosure. Even NGOs and social workers must protect identity. Courts must use initials and not real names in orders and judgments.

Applicable scenarios: Media reporting of abuse cases; Social media disclosure of victim identity; Police records and FIR confidentiality; Court proceedings and identity protection.

## State of Maharashtra v Madhukar Narayan Mardikar (1991) — Victim Character

Case: State of Maharashtra v Madhukar Narayan Mardikar
Year: 1991
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Irrelevance of Victim's Character and Past Sexual History

Principle: Every woman, regardless of her occupation, lifestyle, or past sexual conduct, has the right to sexual privacy and bodily integrity. A woman's character or past sexual history is wholly irrelevant in sexual assault cases and cannot be used to undermine her credibility or testimony. The victim's moral character cannot be put in issue. This principle is now codified in Section 53A of the Indian Evidence Act — no evidence about the victim's past sexual experience is admissible in rape or sexual assault cases.

Applicable scenarios: Cross-examination of survivor; Character evidence in POCSO or POSH proceedings; Victim blaming defence arguments; Two-finger test and character-based evidence attempts.

## Bachpan Bachao Andolan v Union of India (2011) — Child Protection Infrastructure

Case: Bachpan Bachao Andolan v Union of India
Year: 2011
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: State Duty — Child Protection Systems

Principle: The State has a positive constitutional obligation to prevent child abuse and exploitation. Special Juvenile Police Units (SJPUs) must be functional and staffed in every district. Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) must be operational across all districts. The State must ensure that children in conflict with law and children in need of care receive appropriate institutional support. Citizens and NGOs may approach the court for systemic failures in child protection infrastructure.

Applicable scenarios: SJPU non-functioning or failure to act; CWC non-availability; Institutional child abuse; State accountability for child protection failures.

## Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India (2012) — POSH Compliance Enforcement

Case: Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India
Year: 2012
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Enforcement of Vishaka Guidelines and POSH Compliance

Principle: States and Union Territories are constitutionally bound to implement the Vishaka Guidelines (now POSH Act) in letter and spirit. High Courts have jurisdiction to monitor and enforce compliance with workplace sexual harassment norms. Non-compliance by employers and State authorities is punishable. Every government body, private sector employer, and institution must constitute a functioning Complaints Committee. Complaints received must be disposed of within the prescribed time limit.

Applicable scenarios: ICC not constituted; Employer ignoring POSH obligations; Government departments without ICCs; Delayed or biased inquiry proceedings; Enforcing POSH compliance through courts.

## Aparna Bhat v State of Madhya Pradesh (2021) — No Stereotype Conditions

Case: Aparna Bhat v Union of India
Year: 2021
Court: Supreme Court of India
Topic: Dignity of Survivors — Prohibition of Stereotypical Court Conditions

Principle: Courts cannot impose conditions that trivialise, stereotype, or humiliate survivors of sexual offences. Directing an accused to tie rakhi to the survivor, requiring the survivor to forgive the accused, or compelling the survivor to mediate or reconcile with the abuser are unconstitutional and violate the survivor's dignity under Article 21. Such conditions reflect patriarchal assumptions and must never be imposed. Bail conditions must not require or encourage interaction between the accused and the survivor.

Applicable scenarios: Bail conditions in sexual offence cases; Court directions involving the survivor; Mediation and compromise in sexual harassment cases; Judicial stereotyping and dignity violations.
